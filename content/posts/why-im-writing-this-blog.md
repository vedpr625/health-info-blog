## The gap I kept noticing

Interoperability projects generate an enormous amount of knowledge — lessons learned the hard way, trade-offs debated in meeting rooms, workarounds that eventually became best practice. Almost none of it is written down in a form anyone outside the project ever reads. What does get documented is usually formal, sanitized, and locked inside project reports that exist to satisfy a funder, not to teach.

I wanted a place to write the version that doesn't make it into those reports.

## What I've seen across three countries

I've worked on health interoperability in three very different settings: India, Mongolia, and now Sri Lanka, where I'm part of a country-level UNOPS project. Each context has its own constraints — infrastructure, governance maturity, political will, workforce capacity — and yet the underlying challenges rhyme more than you'd expect. Standards adoption, stakeholder alignment, and the gap between a well-designed architecture and a system that actually gets used: these show up everywhere, just wearing different clothes.

## What this blog will cover

I plan to write about:

- **Standards** — HL7, FHIR, and the practical reality of implementing them in resource-constrained settings
- **Governance and policy** — the non-technical hurdles that often decide whether an architecture succeeds
- **Stakeholder management** — getting ministries, implementers, and vendors to agree on anything
- **Technical architecture decisions** — the trade-offs behind the diagrams
- **The human side** — what large-scale health IT projects are actually like to work on

## Who this is for

Informatics professionals, policymakers, fellow architects, and anyone trying to make sense of health interoperability from the inside, rather than from a glossy final report.

## An invitation

If you've worked on similar projects — in Sri Lanka, India, Mongolia, or anywhere else — I'd like to hear how your experience compares. Disagree with something I write here? Good. That's worth a conversation too.
